# BRAIN BENDING - WEEK 6

**Yonathan Zeitoune Mattout - Team 6 - TECHNOLOGIST**

## My angle

The technological question is not whether VR can make an earthquake look real. It can. The harder question is whether a system can rehearse a small number of useful decisions, measure them honestly, and reach people who do not own a headset. The other lenses may see the family, payer, regulation, or rollout. I see a dangerous temptation to confuse visual intensity with training quality.

My opening claim to the model was that Mexico should move directly to headset-based drills because immersion is the point. I changed that position. A headset may deepen presence, but it also moves the bottleneck from content to equipment, facilitation, hygiene, maintenance, and scheduling. The first useful product should run on the phone already in the user's hand and preserve an upgrade path to WebXR.

## The technology, honestly

Today a web product can render an interactive 3D room with WebGL, measure response time and choices locally, and detect whether an immersive WebXR session is supported. The W3C WebXR specification defines inline and immersive session modes, so one codebase can offer a browser view broadly and a headset path selectively. Browser voice recognition can provide optional input, but support is uneven and speech may be processed differently by each browser. It must be capability-detected, opt-in, and never the only control.

Adaptive logic is also possible today, but "ML" should not become theater. We do not have a validated dataset that maps simulator behavior to real earthquake outcomes. For this week the honest version is an explainable adaptive engine: safe decisions remove cues and reduce time; unsafe decisions add one cue and repeat the concept in a changed scene. It adapts practice without claiming to predict survival or certify readiness.

## The world's best attempts - and the border problem

Tokyo's 4DOH is a multi-person 3D disaster theater that adds wind, heat, mist, and smell to virtual scenarios. It gets one thing exactly right: disaster training must be experienced, not merely described. It does not translate directly to Mexican families because it depends on a dedicated installation and supervised throughput.

RoT Studio offers earthquake training in adult MR and child-safe VR modes, including preparation, Drop-Cover-Hold On, communication, and stress control. It gets the scenario sequence and age-specific design right. Crossing into Mexico requires Spanish localization, Mexican housing types, local Civil Protection review, lower-cost delivery, and evidence that the experience changes behavior rather than only confidence.

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security treats VR training systems as a real first-responder procurement category. This validates the tool class, but its buyer, hardware, and operational requirements are far above a household. Mexico's phone base changes the starting point: INEGI reported 84.6% cellphone use in 2025 but computers in only 44.7% of households. The translation is therefore phone-first rehearsal now, optional headset immersion later.

## The vacuum I would attack

I would attack the Family Rehearsal vacuum. Compliance training has a clearer payer, but it pulls the prototype toward institutional checklists before we prove the core behavior loop. A family scenario can test the technical thesis quickly: can three decisions inside one familiar room expose a bad instinct, then improve the second attempt?

The product I would build is 90 Segundos: one 3D apartment, three timed choices, optional voice input, and an explainable adaptive next round. It would measure only what happened inside the rehearsal - choice, delay, and improvement - and never convert that into a universal score.

## The shadow

Chapter 5 shows synthetic experience exploiting grief until simulation becomes emotionally indistinguishable from memory. The parallel risk here is a product that maximizes fear because fear looks like engagement. My design decision is therefore a "minimum necessary intensity" rule: no photoreal injuries, no dead relatives, no surprise use of a real person's likeness, a visible exit at all times, and only three scenarios per session. The system measures decisions, not emotional distress.

## My opening position for Team Bending

I agree that much of the national drill culture becomes theater when success means participation or evacuation time alone. But I reject the absolute claim that one VR school automatically saves more lives than a national drill. That outcome is not yet evidenced. My position is narrower: one measurable, adaptive rehearsal can produce more useful behavioral evidence than one unmeasured routine drill. The build should prove that claim first.
